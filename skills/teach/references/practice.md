# Practice and adaptation

Read when designing a learner task, diagnosing a stuck explanation, or supporting
learning across sessions.

## Choose evidence that distinguishes understanding

Match the task to the capability: ask for a prediction with a reason, a diagnosis
of an example, or an application to a changed case. Repeating the teacher's words
may show recall without showing use. Recognition questions should not reveal
their answer through conspicuous length, wording, or formatting.

Show a worked example when the method is unfamiliar. Then give the learner a
similar problem with less support. Use their attempt to locate the first point
where the reasoning diverges. Explain that point, offer a hint, and invite another
attempt rather than replacing their entire solution.

If they cannot begin, reduce the task or supply the missing prerequisite. If they
can perform the procedure but cannot explain it, ask them to contrast cases or
predict when it fails. If they can explain but cannot execute, practice the action
with feedback. Challenge should make retrieval or judgment useful, not make the
initial explanation needlessly hard to decode.

## Repair the explanation

Locate the likely obstacle from the learner's response. Missing context calls for
setup; unfamiliar language calls for concrete terms; a faulty model calls for a
counterexample or another representation. Use a familiar case to expose the same
relationship, then connect it back to the original question. State limits of
metaphors so their convenient features do not become false rules.

## Support retention when requested

Separate success while an example is visible from recall later without it.
Revisit the capability after a gap, and vary cases once the basic method is
stable. Mix related tasks when choosing the appropriate method is itself part
of the skill.

For ongoing learning, reuse available notes rather than imposing a folder scheme.
Record demonstrated capabilities, corrected misconceptions, open gaps, and the
evidence for them; material merely presented is not material learned. Keep a
compact reference for recurring terms or procedures when useful, and update
earlier simplifications as understanding deepens. Recommend practitioner feedback
when real-world judgment matters, without making community participation a
prerequisite for teaching.
